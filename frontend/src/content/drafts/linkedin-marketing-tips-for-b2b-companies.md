Most B2B LinkedIn strategies fail in the same predictable way: the company page posts three times a week, reaches a few hundred people, generates two likes from employees, and after six months someone concludes that "LinkedIn does not work for our industry."

LinkedIn works. The company page is just the wrong engine to run it on.

This guide covers what actually generates pipeline on LinkedIn for B2B companies — the personal-versus-page reality, content pillars that work for considered purchases, comment strategy, and how to measure it without pretending you have perfect attribution.

## The Structural Truth: Personal Accounts Beat Company Pages

This is the single most important thing to understand, and most B2B marketing plans ignore it.

LinkedIn is built around person-to-person connection. Its distribution consistently favours individual profiles over company pages, and audiences engage far more readily with a named person than with a logo. A founder with 2,000 connections routinely out-reaches a company page with 15,000 followers.

**What to do about it:**

- **The founder or a senior leader should post personally.** This is not optional for early-stage B2B. It is the highest-return marketing activity available.
- **Enable employees to post about their work.** Not scripted advocacy posts — genuine ones about what they are building, learning, or arguing about.
- **Use the company page for credibility, not reach.** Prospects will check it. It should look active and professional. Do not expect it to drive distribution.

The company page is your shopfront. Personal accounts are your salespeople.

## Understanding the B2B Buying Reality

B2B purchases involve multiple stakeholders, long consideration periods, and a buyer who is doing most of their research before ever speaking to you. Some of the people who eventually approve your deal will have seen your content for months without ever engaging with it.

Three consequences that should shape everything you publish:

**Most of your audience is silent.** Engagement rates on B2B content look poor compared to consumer content, and that is normal. The buyer who reads every post but never likes anything is your most valuable reader.

**You are building familiarity, not closing deals.** The job of a LinkedIn post is to make you the obvious person to call when the problem becomes urgent. That is a slow accumulation, not a conversion event.

**Your content must survive being read by a sceptic.** B2B audiences are professionally cautious. Vague claims and unverified statistics do real damage to credibility here.

## Four Content Pillars That Work for B2B

Rotate through these. Each does a different job in a long buying cycle.

### 1. The problem, described precisely

Content that describes a problem your buyer has — accurately enough that they think "how does this person know that?"

This is the highest-value B2B content and the most underused. You do not need to solve it in the post. Naming the problem precisely is what establishes that you understand their world.

*"Your sales team is not using the CRM properly. It is not a training issue — it is that you are asking them to enter data that only benefits management."*

### 2. The contrarian position

Where you disagree with standard practice in your industry, and why.

B2B feeds are full of agreeable, uncontroversial content. A defensible contrarian position gets read, remembered, and argued with — and arguments are comments.

Be genuinely defensible. Manufactured controversy is transparent and reputationally costly.

### 3. Proof and specifics

Real numbers, real cases, real outcomes.

- A result you achieved, with the actual figures
- A project post-mortem, including what went wrong
- A comparison you ran, with data
- What your customers actually asked in the last month

**Verify every number.** In B2B, where credibility is the product, a fabricated or careless statistic is worse than posting nothing. This is also why AI-generated statistics are particularly dangerous here — always trace figures to a source.

### 4. Process and method

How you actually do the work. Frameworks, checklists, decision criteria.

This earns saves, which is the strongest signal available, and it does the useful work of pre-qualifying prospects — people who read your method and want it will arrive already convinced.

## Writing Posts That Get Read

The structural rules matter as much as the substance.

**The first two lines are everything.** Before "see more," you have roughly 140–200 characters. Lead with a number, a contrarian claim, or a named mistake. Never a rhetorical question.

**Format for phones.** One to three sentences per paragraph. A blank line between each. Dense text gets abandoned regardless of quality.

**Length: roughly 800–1,500 characters** for most posts. Longer only when the substance earns it.

**End with a real question.** Not "Agree?" — something you actually want answered. *"Curious whether other teams are seeing this, or whether it is specific to our segment."*

**Keep links out of the post body.** External links generally reduce distribution. Put them in the first comment.

## Comment Strategy: The Underrated Half

Most B2B companies treat commenting as an afterthought. It is often more valuable than posting.

**Comment on your prospects' posts, genuinely.** Not "Great insight!" — an actual contribution that adds something. This puts you in front of exactly the right people, repeatedly, without pitching. It is the closest thing to free, targeted, welcome exposure that exists.

**Reply to every comment on your own posts, within the first hour where possible.** Early conversation strongly influences distribution, and it makes people more likely to engage next time.

**Treat comment threads as content.** A thoughtful reply to a challenging comment is often read by more people than the original post.

Fifteen minutes a day of substantive commenting will usually do more for a B2B company than an extra post per week.

## Where AI Helps — and Where It Does Not

**Genuinely useful:**

- **Hook variations.** Generate ten openings for each post; keep the best. Fast, reliable improvement.
- **Restructuring drafts** into readable, white-space-heavy formatting.
- **Turning raw thought into structure.** Dictate three minutes about a client situation, ask AI to shape it. Substance stays yours.
- **Repurposing** a case study, webinar, or report into eight or ten distinct posts. See our [repurposing guide](/blog/repurpose-one-content-into-10-social-posts).
- **Drafting comment replies** when a post takes off — reviewed and sent by you.

**Actively harmful in B2B:**

- **Generating posts from bare topics.** Produces exactly the interchangeable content that makes B2B feeds unreadable, and your buyers are especially good at spotting it.
- **Inventing statistics.** The credibility damage in B2B is disproportionate.
- **Automated commenting or connection messages.** Immediately recognisable and reputationally costly with a professional audience.
- **Softening your position.** AI defaults to balanced. Balanced does not travel.

## Measuring Without Fooling Yourself

B2B attribution on LinkedIn is genuinely difficult, and elaborate tracking usually creates false confidence rather than insight.

**Metrics worth watching:**

- **Profile views** on the personal accounts posting. Rising profile views mean people are checking who you are — a genuine buying signal.
- **Saves and shares**, over likes.
- **Comment quality.** Substantive comments from people who look like your buyer are worth more than a hundred likes.
- **Inbound enquiries mentioning your content.** Unfashionable but decisive.
- **"How did you hear about us?"** on every enquiry form and first call.

**Metrics to largely ignore:** company page follower count, impressions in isolation, and any single post's performance. Variance is high; look at direction over quarters.

**Set realistic timelines.** B2B LinkedIn is a compounding channel. Expect familiarity in months one to three, inbound conversations mentioning your content around months four to six, and meaningful pipeline contribution after that. Companies that judge it at week eight always conclude it does not work.

## Common B2B LinkedIn Mistakes

- **Relying on the company page for reach.** The structural mistake underneath most failed B2B LinkedIn efforts.
- **Corporate register.** Press-release language performs badly. Write how you would explain it to a peer.
- **Announcement content.** "We are pleased to announce" is not content. Nobody outside your company cares about your milestones unless you make them about their problem.
- **Posting and leaving.** The first hour is when comments matter most.
- **Gated everything.** Making all substance sit behind a form limits the familiarity-building that LinkedIn is actually good at. Give away the thinking; sell the doing.
- **Automated connection requests with a pitch.** Widely disliked, immediately obvious, and it burns the account's reputation.
- **Being agreeable.** Content nobody could disagree with generates no discussion and is instantly forgotten.
- **Inconsistency.** Two posts a week for a year beats daily posting for six weeks.

## A Realistic Weekly Operating Rhythm

For a small B2B company:

- **Founder or lead: 2–3 personal posts per week.** Drawn from real client situations, captured in a running note.
- **Company page: 2 posts per week.** Reshare and adapt personal content, plus credibility content.
- **15 minutes daily: substantive comments** on prospects' and peers' posts.
- **First hour after each post: reply to everything.**
- **Monthly: one substantial asset** — a case study, report, or recorded conversation — repurposed across the following month.
- **Quarterly: review** which pillars generated profile views and enquiries; cut what did not.

That is roughly three to four hours a week, most of it commenting and replying rather than writing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should B2B companies post from the company page or personal profiles?

Both, but personal profiles do the heavy lifting. LinkedIn's distribution favours person-to-person connection, so a founder with a modest network routinely out-reaches a company page with far more followers. Use personal accounts — the founder's and employees' — for reach and relationship-building, and keep the company page active and professional for credibility, since prospects will check it.

### How often should a B2B company post on LinkedIn?

Two to three personal posts a week from key people, sustained indefinitely, works better than higher-frequency posting that collapses. B2B buying cycles are long, so consistency over quarters matters far more than volume in any given week. Add fifteen minutes a day of genuine commenting on other people's posts — that often produces more than an extra post would.

### How long does LinkedIn take to generate B2B leads?

Longer than most plans allow for. A realistic pattern is growing familiarity and profile views in the first one to three months, inbound conversations that reference your content around months four to six, and meaningful pipeline contribution after that. Most B2B companies that judge LinkedIn at week eight conclude it does not work, when the channel simply had not compounded yet.

### What should B2B companies post about on LinkedIn?

Four pillars cover it: precisely described customer problems, defensible contrarian positions on industry practice, specific proof with real numbers and cases, and your actual method or framework. What consistently fails is announcement content about company milestones, which matters to you and almost nobody else unless you connect it directly to a buyer's problem.

### Is it worth using AI for LinkedIn content in B2B?

For structure, hooks, formatting, and repurposing — yes, and it saves considerable time. For generating posts from bare topics — no. B2B audiences are unusually good at recognising generic content, and credibility is the product you are selling. The workflow that works is supplying your own raw material about real client situations and using AI to shape it, then verifying every number before publishing.

## The Bottom Line

B2B LinkedIn is not a broadcast channel. It is a familiarity engine, and it runs on people rather than pages.

Get your founder posting personally. Write about problems precisely, take positions, show real numbers. Spend as much time commenting as posting. Measure profile views and inbound mentions, not follower count. Give it two quarters before judging it.

Do that and LinkedIn becomes the channel where your buyers already know who you are before they ever fill in a form.
