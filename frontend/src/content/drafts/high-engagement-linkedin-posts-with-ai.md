LinkedIn rewards a very specific kind of writing, and most business posts fail on the same three things: a weak first line, a wall of text, and no actual point of view.

The good news is that all three are fixable, and AI is genuinely useful for two of them. The catch is that AI is also responsible for a flood of interchangeable LinkedIn content — the posts that open with a rhetorical question, contain three tidy bullet points, and say absolutely nothing. Publishing more of that will not help you.

This guide covers what actually makes LinkedIn posts perform, exactly where AI helps, and how to use it without producing the thing everyone is already tired of reading.

## How LinkedIn Distribution Actually Works

You do not need to model the ranking system precisely, but a rough mental picture changes how you write.

When you publish, LinkedIn shows the post to a portion of your network. What happens in the first hour or two — whether people stop, expand, comment, and react — largely determines how much further it travels. Comments and reshares generally signal more than reactions do, and comments with substance signal more than "Great post!"

Three practical consequences:

- **The first two lines carry disproportionate weight**, because they are all anyone sees before deciding to click "see more."
- **Dwell time matters.** A post someone actually reads beats one they scroll past, even with the same number of likes.
- **Early conversation compounds.** Replying to comments in the first hour is one of the highest-return things you can do.

## The Anatomy of a LinkedIn Post That Works

### The hook: your first two lines

This is where the post is won or lost. Before the "see more" cut, you have roughly 140–200 characters visible on most screens.

Hooks that consistently work:

- **A specific number or result.** "We cut our onboarding time from 6 weeks to 9 days. Here is what actually did it."
- **A contrarian position.** "Most B2B content calendars are a waste of time. Ours was, for two years."
- **A named mistake.** "I lost a £40k deal because of one line in a proposal."
- **A direct, useful promise.** "Five questions to ask before signing any SaaS contract."

Hooks that reliably fail:

- Rhetorical questions. "Ever wondered how to grow your business?"
- Throat-clearing. "I have been thinking a lot about leadership lately."
- Generic assertions. "Consistency is key to success."
- Anything beginning "In today's fast-paced digital landscape."

That last set is exactly what unedited AI produces by default. Which is the whole reason this guide exists.

### The body: white space is a feature

LinkedIn is read on phones, in gaps between other things. Dense paragraphs get abandoned.

- One to three sentences per paragraph, maximum.
- A blank line between every paragraph.
- Short sentences. Then occasionally a longer one for rhythm.
- Total length between roughly 800 and 1,500 characters for most posts. Longer works when the substance genuinely earns it.

### The substance: the part AI cannot supply

This is what separates a post that travels from one that does not.

Every strong LinkedIn post contains at least one of:

- A **number from your own experience** — not an industry statistic, yours.
- A **specific situation** with enough detail to be real. A client, a deadline, a decision.
- An **opinion someone could disagree with.** If nobody could argue, nobody will comment.
- A **mistake you made.** Reliably the highest-engagement category, and the least used.

### The close: earn the comment

End with a genuine question or a clear takeaway. Avoid engagement bait — "Agree? 👇" is transparent and increasingly ignored.

Better: "What would you have done differently?" or "Curious whether this matches what others are seeing."

## Where AI Genuinely Helps

Now the practical part — the specific jobs to hand over.

### 1. Generating hook variations

This is AI's best LinkedIn use case. Write your post, then ask for ten alternative opening lines in different styles — number-led, contrarian, mistake-led, question-led.

You will discard eight. One or two will be better than what you wrote, and finding them took ninety seconds.

### 2. Restructuring for readability

Paste a dense draft and ask for it to be reformatted into short paragraphs with white space, without changing the meaning. This is a mechanical task AI does perfectly and humans find tedious.

### 3. Turning raw material into structure

The highest-value workflow: you supply the substance, AI supplies the shape.

Record or type three minutes of unstructured thoughts about something that actually happened — a client conversation, a decision, a result. Then ask AI to structure it as a LinkedIn post with a strong hook, short paragraphs, and a closing question.

The substance is entirely yours. The formatting labour disappears. This produces dramatically better posts than asking AI to write about a topic from scratch.

### 4. Repurposing long-form content

One blog post, webinar, or case study contains many LinkedIn posts — a statistic, a contrarian take, a step-by-step, a mistake, a customer quote. AI extracts these reliably because the source supplies the substance. See [repurposing one piece of content into ten social posts](/blog/repurpose-one-content-into-10-social-posts).

### 5. Adapting one idea across platforms

If a post performs on LinkedIn, adapting it for other networks is one instruction rather than three writing tasks.

### 6. Drafting comment replies

Not sending them — drafting. When a post takes off and you have forty comments to answer within the hour, AI-drafted replies you then personalise are the difference between responding to all of them and responding to none.

## Where AI Actively Hurts

- **Writing the whole post from a topic.** "Write a LinkedIn post about leadership" produces exactly the content everyone is tired of. There is no substance to work with, so it generates platitudes.
- **Inventing statistics.** AI will produce a confident, specific, non-existent figure. On LinkedIn, where your professional credibility is the product, this is genuinely damaging. Verify every number.
- **Fabricating personal stories.** Do not let AI invent a client anecdote. Beyond the honesty problem, invented stories lack the odd specific details that make real ones believable.
- **Setting your opinion.** AI defaults to balanced, agreeable positions. Balanced positions do not travel on LinkedIn. Take a side.
- **Auto-commenting.** Automated comments on other people's posts are obvious and reputationally costly.

## A Repeatable Weekly Workflow

Roughly forty-five minutes a week for three or four solid posts.

**Capture (ongoing, 0 minutes of dedicated time).** Keep one note. Every time a client asks a good question, you disagree with something in a meeting, or a project produces a real number — write one line. Arrive at your writing session with raw material rather than a blank page.

**Draft (20 minutes).** Take four items from the note. For each, dictate or type three minutes of unstructured thought. Ask AI to structure each into a LinkedIn post. Do not edit yet.

**Sharpen (15 minutes).** Now edit all four:

- Generate ten hook options for each and pick the strongest.
- Cut every sentence that does not carry information.
- Verify any number.
- Check there is a real opinion in there. If it is inoffensive, sharpen it.

**Schedule (5 minutes).** Space them across the week — for most people, three posts on separate days outperforms three in one day.

**Engage (10 minutes per post, on the day).** Be present for the first hour. Reply properly to every comment. This is not optional and it is not automatable.

## What to Post About When You Think You Have Nothing

The most common blocker is believing your work is too ordinary to write about. It almost never is — you are simply too close to it.

Five sources that reliably produce material:

- **A question a client asked this week.** If one person asked, several are wondering. The answer is a post.
- **Something you disagreed with in a meeting.** Disagreement is a position, and positions travel.
- **A decision you made and the reasoning.** Not the outcome — the reasoning. People find decision-making more interesting than results.
- **A number from your own work.** Any real figure, even an unimpressive one. *"We tried this for six months and it moved nothing"* is a genuinely good post.
- **A mistake, while it is still slightly uncomfortable.** The discomfort is the signal that it is worth publishing.

Keep one running note and add a single line whenever one of these happens. Arriving at a writing session with fifteen raw prompts is an entirely different task from arriving with a blank page — and it is the difference between posting weekly and posting occasionally.

## Common Mistakes

- **Publishing unedited AI output.** LinkedIn audiences are unusually good at spotting it, and it damages professional credibility more than on other platforms.
- **Posting from the company page only.** Personal profiles consistently reach further. If you are a founder, post from your own account.
- **External links in the post body.** Distribution generally suffers. Put the link in the first comment or leave it out.
- **Being agreeable.** Posts nobody could disagree with generate no discussion.
- **Publishing and leaving.** The first hour is when comments matter most. Be there.
- **Formatting like a press release.** Corporate register performs badly. Write how you would explain it to a colleague.
- **Overusing formatting tricks.** Excessive emoji bullets and unicode fonts read as spam and hurt accessibility.

## Two Before-and-After Examples

**Before:** "In today's competitive business environment, effective client onboarding is more important than ever. Here are three tips to improve your onboarding process."

**After:** "We were losing 1 in 4 new clients in the first month. The problem was not our product — it was that our onboarding took six weeks and they lost momentum. Here is what we changed."

**Before:** "Excited to share that our team has been working hard on some exciting new developments!"

**After:** "We spent four months building a feature nobody used. Here is the question we should have asked in week one."

The difference in both cases is specificity and a real stake. AI can produce the second version — but only when you supply the underlying facts.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can AI write LinkedIn posts that actually get engagement?

AI can write the *structure* of a high-performing post reliably, but it cannot supply the substance that drives engagement — your specific numbers, real situations, and genuine opinions. The workflow that works is supplying three minutes of raw thought about something that actually happened and asking AI to shape it. Asking AI to write about a topic from scratch produces the generic content LinkedIn audiences now scroll past.

### How long should a LinkedIn post be?

Most well-performing posts land between roughly 800 and 1,500 characters, formatted as short paragraphs with a blank line between each. Length matters far less than structure — a 1,400-character post in three-line paragraphs reads faster than a 700-character wall of text. Go longer only when the substance genuinely earns it.

### Does LinkedIn reduce reach for posts with external links?

Posts containing external links in the body generally see lower distribution than those without, since the platform favours content that keeps people on-site. The common workaround is putting the link in the first comment and referencing it in the post. Test it for your own account rather than treating it as an absolute rule, since platform behaviour changes.

### Should I post from my personal profile or my company page?

Both, with different expectations. Personal profiles consistently reach further because LinkedIn favours person-to-person distribution and audiences engage more with individuals than logos. Use your company page for credibility, recruitment, and as a reference point; use personal accounts — yours and your team's — for actual reach.

### How often should I post on LinkedIn?

Three to five times a week works well for most professionals, but consistency matters more than frequency. Two genuinely substantial posts a week, sustained for a year, outperform daily posting that collapses after six weeks. Start at a cadence you can maintain during your busiest month, not your calmest one.

## The Short Version

LinkedIn does not reward volume, polish, or clever wording. It rewards a strong first line, readable formatting, and something specific enough that a real person wants to respond.

Use AI for the hooks, the structure, the formatting, and the repurposing. Supply the numbers, the stories, and the opinions yourself. Then show up in the comments for an hour.

That combination produces posts that travel — and it takes about forty-five minutes a week.
