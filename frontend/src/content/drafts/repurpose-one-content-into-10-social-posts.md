Most businesses publish something substantial — a blog post, a webinar, a case study — share it once, and move on.

That single share reaches whichever fraction of your audience happened to be online in that hour. The other 90% never see it. Meanwhile you go back to a blank page to write next week's posts, sitting on an asset that already contains a fortnight of content.

Repurposing fixes this. Not by reposting the same link with different wording, but by extracting genuinely different pieces from the same source. This guide gives you ten specific formats, worked through with a real example, plus the mistakes that make repurposing look lazy rather than efficient.

## Why Repurposing Works

Three reasons, and the third is the one people miss.

**Reach is fragmented.** Any given post reaches a slice of your followers. Publishing five angles from one source over three weeks reaches far more people than publishing once, and almost nobody sees all five.

**Different formats reach different people.** Some of your audience reads carousels. Some only watch video. Some engage with text posts and ignore everything visual. The same idea in four formats is not repetition — it is coverage.

**The substance already exists.** This is the crucial one for AI-assisted work. When you ask AI to write about a topic from nothing, it produces generic content. When you ask it to extract a specific angle from *your* article — with your data, your examples, your opinion — the substance is already yours. Repurposing is the single best use case for AI content generation, because it solves the input-quality problem by definition.

## What Makes a Good Source

Not everything is worth repurposing. Good sources have:

- **Multiple distinct points**, not one idea stretched thin
- **At least one number or concrete result**
- **A real example or story**
- **An opinion** that someone could reasonably disagree with

Strong candidates: a detailed blog post, a webinar recording, a customer case study, a podcast episode, a conference talk, a long internal document, even a thorough email you wrote to a client.

Weak candidates: a news roundup, a short announcement, anything that is essentially one sentence.

## The Ten Formats

Work through these in order. Each pulls a different piece from the same source.

### 1. The core insight

The single most valuable thing in the piece, stated plainly, with the reasoning compressed to three or four sentences.

This is not a summary. Summaries are boring. Take the strongest claim, state it directly, and give the one reason it is true.

*Source line:* "Our data showed that clients who completed onboarding within ten days renewed at a much higher rate."
*Post:* the claim, why it happens, one implication for the reader.

### 2. The statistic post

Lead with a number, then explain what it means.

Numbers stop scrolling because they are concrete. Use a number from your own source — that is what makes it yours rather than a recycled industry statistic. Verify it before publishing.

### 3. The contrarian take

Find where your source disagrees with conventional wisdom and lead with that.

This is consistently the highest-engagement format because it invites disagreement, and disagreement produces comments. If your piece contains no contrarian element, you may be publishing safe content generally.

*"Most onboarding advice says be thorough. We cut ours in half and retention improved."*

### 4. The step-by-step

Extract the process, method, or framework as numbered steps.

Practical, reference-worthy content is what people *save*, and saves are weighted heavily by most platforms. Works particularly well as a carousel.

### 5. The mistake post

What did you or your customers get wrong? What does the piece warn against?

Mistake-framed content outperforms advice-framed content reliably, because it is more specific and lower-risk to read. Nobody feels lectured by "here is what we got wrong."

### 6. The customer story

Pull any example, case, or anecdote and tell it properly: situation, what changed, result.

Keep the odd specific details — the ones that make it clearly real rather than illustrative. Anonymise if needed, but do not sand off the texture.

### 7. The question to your audience

Take a point of tension from the piece and ask what others do.

Genuine questions, not engagement bait. "How long is your onboarding? Ours was six weeks and it was killing us" invites real answers.

### 8. The quote card

A single strong sentence, set as a visual.

Works when the sentence genuinely stands alone. If it needs context to make sense, it is not a quote card.

### 9. The myth or misconception

What does your audience believe that your source shows is wrong?

Similar to the contrarian take, but framed around a belief rather than an opinion. Slightly safer, still engaging.

### 10. The short video or talking head

Record yourself explaining one point from the piece in sixty seconds. No script, no editing, phone camera.

This consistently outperforms polished video in B2B and local business contexts, and it takes three minutes to make.

## A Worked Example

**Source:** a 1,500-word blog post from a B2B software company titled "Why our onboarding was losing us customers."

The article contains: a retention figure before and after, a six-week to nine-day timeline change, three specific things they cut, one customer quote, and the opinion that most onboarding is designed for the vendor rather than the customer.

Ten posts from it:

1. **Core insight** — "Onboarding is not a training problem, it is a momentum problem."
2. **Statistic** — the before-and-after retention number, and what caused the gap.
3. **Contrarian** — "We made our onboarding worse on paper and our retention went up."
4. **Step-by-step** — the five things they now do in the first nine days.
5. **Mistake** — "We spent two years optimising the wrong part of onboarding."
6. **Customer story** — the quoted customer's experience, told properly.
7. **Question** — "How long is your onboarding, honestly? We thought six weeks was normal."
8. **Quote card** — "Most onboarding is designed for the vendor, not the customer."
9. **Myth** — "Myth: more thorough onboarding means better retention."
10. **Video** — sixty seconds explaining the momentum point to camera.

Spread across three weeks, on two platforms with platform-specific rewrites, that is roughly a month of content from one article. Nobody perceives it as repetition, because each post makes a genuinely different point.

## Using AI for This Properly

The workflow that works:

**Step 1: extract, do not generate.** Paste the full source and ask for the ten angles listed above as *topics* first — not finished posts. Review them. Cut what does not work, and add anything the AI missed that you know matters.

**Step 2: draft in one pass.** Generate all approved angles together, including platform variants. Ask for your brand voice explicitly and supply an example post if you have one.

**Step 3: edit.** Standard five-minute pass per post. The critical check here is **verify every number** — including numbers that appear in your source, since AI sometimes alters them in transit. This happens more often than people expect.

**Step 4: schedule with spacing.** Never publish repurposed posts back to back. Spread them across two to four weeks, mixed with other content.

## Repurposing Across Time, Not Just Formats

Most repurposing advice stops at "one piece into many formats." The second dimension — reusing the same source months later — is where the compounding actually happens.

Your audience turns over. People who follow you in September never saw what you published in March, and the people who did have forgotten it. A strong idea is not spent after one run.

**A workable rotation:**

- **Month 0:** publish the source and extract eight to twelve posts across three to four weeks.
- **Month 6:** revisit the two best-performing angles from a different direction. What changed? What would you say differently now? Has the number moved?
- **Month 12:** if it is genuinely evergreen, update the source itself and run the whole extraction again.

**Two rules that keep this honest.** Never republish the identical post — always change the angle, the hook, or the framing. And check whether anything has become inaccurate; a recycled post with an outdated figure is worse than no post.

Keep a simple log of what you have repurposed and when. Without it you will either repeat yourself too soon or forget your best material entirely.

## Common Mistakes

- **Publishing them too close together.** Five posts from one article in one week reads as repetitive. Across a month, it reads as consistency.
- **Summarising rather than extracting.** Ten summaries of the same article are ten versions of the same post. Ten *angles* are ten different posts.
- **Keeping the same hook.** If every post opens the same way, the repurposing is visible. Vary the opening deliberately.
- **Repurposing weak sources.** If the original had one idea, you will get one post and nine paddings.
- **Losing the specifics in translation.** The details are what make it yours. If a post could have come from any competitor, put a specific back in.
- **Only ever repurposing.** You still need fresh material. Repurposing amplifies your best assets; it does not replace creating new ones.
- **Forgetting to link back.** Some of these posts should drive traffic to the original. Not all — but some.

## Building a Repurposing Habit

Three things make it systematic rather than occasional.

**Create one substantial asset per month.** A detailed blog post, a recorded conversation, a case study. That single asset supplies most of the following month's social content, which changes the economics of producing it entirely.

**Keep a source library.** A simple list of everything worth repurposing, with a note on what has already been used. Good sources can be repurposed again after six months with a fresh angle.

**Repurpose your best performers, twice.** If a post did unusually well, that idea has demand. Revisit it a few months later from a different angle rather than assuming it is used up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is repurposing content bad for reach or considered duplicate content?

No. Duplicate content concerns apply to identical pages competing in web search, not to social posts that share a theme. On social platforms, only a fraction of your audience sees any given post, so covering one topic from several angles increases coverage rather than cannibalising it. What does hurt is publishing near-identical text repeatedly — vary the angle, not just the wording.

### How long should I wait between repurposed posts from the same source?

Space them across two to four weeks and mix other content in between. Publishing five posts from one article in a single week reads as repetitive; the same five spread across a month reads as a consistent point of view. If you repurpose across multiple platforms, you can run them closer together since the audiences overlap less.

### What kind of content is best to repurpose?

Anything with multiple distinct points, at least one real number, a concrete example, and an opinion — detailed blog posts, webinars, case studies, podcast episodes, and conference talks all qualify. Short announcements and news roundups make poor sources because there is only one idea to extract, and stretching it produces nine filler posts.

### Can AI repurpose content accurately without changing the facts?

Mostly, but not reliably enough to skip checking. AI occasionally alters figures, dates, or attributions when reshaping text, which is easy to miss because the output reads confidently. Always verify numbers against the original source before publishing — especially in the statistic post, where the number is the entire point.

### How many social posts can I realistically get from one blog article?

A substantial 1,500-word article with real data and examples reliably yields eight to twelve distinct posts before quality drops. Multiply that by platform variants and one article can cover most of a month. A thin or single-idea article will yield two or three good posts and should not be forced further.

## Wrapping Up

Repurposing is not a shortcut. It is the recognition that you already did the hard thinking, and publishing it once was the waste.

Pick your best piece from the last six months. Run it through the ten formats. Space the results across a month. You will have a fortnight of specific, on-brand content that took an afternoon — and it will outperform generic content written from scratch, because the substance was yours to begin with.
