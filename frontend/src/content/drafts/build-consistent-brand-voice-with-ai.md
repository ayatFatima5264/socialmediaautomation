"Make it sound like us" is the instruction every AI tool fails at, and it fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the technology.

Most businesses cannot actually describe how they sound. Ask for a brand voice and you get three adjectives — "professional, friendly, approachable" — that describe roughly 90% of all companies. Feed those to an AI and it produces generic output, because generic is precisely what you specified.

This guide fixes that. It covers the four dimensions that actually define a voice, how to write a brief AI can follow, a reusable voice prompt, and a scoring test you can run on any generated draft in thirty seconds.

## Why Adjectives Do Not Work

"Professional and friendly" is not a specification. It is a hope.

Consider two businesses that would both describe themselves that way:

*"We're sorry your order hasn't arrived. We've raised this with our logistics partner and will update you within 24 hours."*

*"Ugh, sorry — that's frustrating. We've chased it up and I'll come back to you tomorrow with an actual answer."*

Both are professional. Both are friendly. They are completely different brands, and no combination of adjectives distinguishes them.

What distinguishes them is a set of concrete choices about formality, energy, warmth, and directness. Those are specifiable, and once specified, an AI can follow them consistently — often more consistently than a team of three human writers.

## The Four Dimensions

Position your brand on each of these, then write one example sentence demonstrating it. The examples matter more than the positions.

### 1. Formality

Where do you sit between formal and casual?

- Do you use contractions? "We cannot" versus "we can't."
- First person or third? "We think" versus "The company believes."
- Do you address the reader as "you" or as "customers"?
- Any slang, idiom, or regional expression? Which specifically?

*Example sentence: "We can't promise it'll be quick, but we can promise you'll know what's happening."*

### 2. Energy

How much intensity do you carry?

- Exclamation marks: never, sparingly, or freely?
- Sentence length: short and punchy, or measured and flowing?
- Emphasis: do you use superlatives, or understate?
- Emoji: none, occasional, or integral?

*Example sentence: "This is a genuinely good result. Not a record — just solid, repeatable, and finally predictable."*

### 3. Warmth

How much personal connection do you show?

- Do you acknowledge feelings, or stay task-focused?
- Do you use humour? What kind — dry, warm, self-deprecating, never?
- Do you talk about your team as people, or as a company?
- Do you admit mistakes openly?

*Example sentence: "We got this wrong last month. Here's what happened and what we've changed."*

### 4. Directness

How plainly do you state things?

- Do you hedge and qualify, or commit to positions?
- Do you lead with the conclusion or build to it?
- Do you use jargon, or translate it?
- Do you tell customers when something is not right for them?

*Example sentence: "If you're posting twice a month, this won't help. Fix consistency first."*

## Writing a Brief AI Can Actually Follow

Assemble the four dimensions into a document with these five sections.

**1. Positioning on each dimension**, with your example sentence for each.

**2. Words you use.** Every business has a vocabulary. Do you say "customers," "clients," "members," or "users"? "Book," "order," or "reserve"? Write down ten to fifteen terms.

**3. Words you never use.** Often more useful than the first list. Common entries: "solutions," "leverage," "seamless," "revolutionise," "game-changing," "in today's landscape," "we are excited to announce."

**4. Three example passages** of your own writing that you consider on-voice. This is the highest-leverage part of the whole document. Voice is far easier to demonstrate than to describe, and AI matches examples much better than it matches adjectives.

**5. Three anti-examples** — passages that are technically fine but sound wrong for you, with a one-line note on why.

That is one page. It will outperform any amount of prompt engineering.

## A Reusable Voice Prompt

Once the brief exists, use it consistently. A workable structure:

> Write in our brand voice. **Formality:** we use contractions, first person plural, and address the reader as "you." **Energy:** measured, no exclamation marks, understate rather than overstate. **Warmth:** we acknowledge difficulty, use dry humour occasionally, and admit mistakes openly. **Directness:** we lead with the conclusion, avoid hedging, and tell people when something is not right for them.
>
> **Never use:** "solutions," "leverage," "seamless," "in today's landscape," "we are excited to announce."
>
> **Match the register of this example:** [paste one of your on-voice passages]

The example at the end does more work than everything above it. Include it every time.

Better still, store this in a tool that keeps your brand context so you are not pasting it into every prompt — that repetition is the main reason people abandon voice consistency.

## The Thirty-Second Scoring Test

Run this on any generated draft before publishing. Score each out of 2.

1. **Formality match** — contractions, person, and address correct? *(0–2)*
2. **Energy match** — sentence rhythm and intensity right? *(0–2)*
3. **Warmth match** — the right amount of humanity, not more or less? *(0–2)*
4. **Directness match** — does it commit, or hedge? *(0–2)*
5. **Banned words** — any present? *(0 if yes, 2 if clean)*

**8–10:** publish after a normal edit.
**5–7:** fix the specific dimension that scored low. Usually one is dragging.
**Below 5:** your brief needs more examples, not a better prompt.

Running this ten times teaches you which dimension your AI consistently misses, and you can then front-load that in your prompt.

## Maintaining Voice as You Scale

Voice drifts. It drifts with multiple writers, with new tools, and simply with time.

**Feed your best work back in.** Every quarter, replace your three example passages with your three best recent pieces. Voice evolves; the examples should evolve with it.

**Run a quarterly audit.** Read twenty recent posts in one sitting. Drift is invisible day to day and glaring in bulk — you will notice a tone that has slid formal, or a phrase you now overuse.

**Give the brief to every new person and tool.** Including freelancers, agencies, and any new AI tool. The document is the standard, not the person who wrote it.

**Watch for creeping banned words.** They return. "Solutions" is remarkably persistent.

## Common Mistakes

- **Adjectives instead of examples.** The root cause of nearly every "AI does not sound like us" complaint.
- **No banned word list.** Removing what is wrong is often faster than specifying what is right.
- **A voice nobody actually wants to read.** Some businesses specify a corporate voice and then wonder why engagement is low. Voice should be distinctive, not merely correct.
- **Different voices per platform.** Adapt *format* per platform, not personality. You should be recognisably the same business on LinkedIn and Instagram.
- **Setting it once and never revisiting.** Your positioning changes; the brief should too.
- **Confusing voice with tone.** Voice is constant. Tone shifts with context — the same brand is warmer in a celebration post and more measured in an apology.
- **Over-specifying.** A page is enough. A twenty-page brand bible gets ignored by humans and truncated by tools.

## Voice Versus Tone: Knowing When to Bend

Voice is constant. Tone shifts with context — and knowing when to shift is the judgment AI cannot exercise for you.

The same business should sound recognisably itself in a celebration post and an apology, but not identical. Three situations where tone must move:

- **Apologies and complaints.** Drop the humour entirely. Increase directness. Never let a scheduled or AI-drafted reply handle these.
- **Genuinely serious news.** Redundancies, outages, safety issues. Formality goes up, energy goes down, and the usual personality steps back.
- **Celebration.** Warmth and energy can rise well above baseline. This is the one direction where over-correcting is rarely a problem.

Write a line in your brief for each of these. It takes two minutes and it prevents the single most damaging voice failure — a chirpy, on-brand post published during a moment that called for seriousness.

## Getting a Team to Actually Use It

A brand voice document that lives in a folder nobody opens is worth nothing. Three things make it stick:

**Put it where the work happens.** Pinned in the channel where content gets drafted, or stored directly in the tool that generates it. A document people have to go looking for will not be used.

**Make the banned-word list the entry point.** It is the shortest, most concrete, most immediately useful part. People adopt it first and then read the rest.

**Review drafts against the score, not against opinion.** "This scores low on directness — it hedges three times in the first paragraph" is actionable feedback. "This doesn't feel like us" starts an argument nobody can win.

## A Worked Example

A small accountancy practice. Their original brief said "professional, trustworthy, approachable" — which produced entirely generic output.

Their rewritten brief:

- **Formality:** contractions yes, "we" not "the firm," address clients as "you." Never "shall" or "hereby."
- **Energy:** calm and measured. No exclamation marks. Short sentences. We do not do urgency.
- **Warmth:** we acknowledge that tax is stressful. Dry humour occasionally. We use our actual names.
- **Directness:** we lead with the answer, then explain. We say when something is not worth doing.
- **Never:** "solutions," "bespoke," "leverage," "value-add," "reach out."
- **Example:** *"You probably don't need to incorporate yet. Here's the threshold where it starts to make sense, and what changes when you cross it."*

That last sentence tells an AI more than three paragraphs of adjectives, and their generated drafts went from unusable to needing a light edit.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I define my brand voice for AI tools?

Skip adjectives and specify four concrete dimensions instead: formality (contractions, person, how you address readers), energy (sentence rhythm, punctuation, emphasis), warmth (humour, acknowledging feelings, admitting mistakes), and directness (hedging versus committing). Write one example sentence for each, add a list of words you never use, and include three passages of your own writing as reference. The examples do most of the work.

### Why does my AI content not sound like my brand?

Almost always because the input describes your voice in adjectives rather than demonstrating it. "Professional and friendly" applies to nearly every business, so the AI produces the average of all professional, friendly writing — which is generic by definition. Adding two or three passages of your actual writing as reference examples produces a bigger improvement than any amount of prompt refinement.

### Should brand voice change between social media platforms?

Format should change; personality should not. A LinkedIn post needs a different structure and register from an Instagram caption, but the underlying voice — how formal you are, whether you hedge, whether you use humour — should be recognisably the same. If a customer reads both and cannot tell they came from the same business, you have gone too far.

### How often should I update my brand voice guidelines?

Review quarterly and refresh the example passages with your best recent work, since voice evolves naturally as a business matures. A full rewrite is only needed when your positioning genuinely changes — a new market, a new audience, a repositioning. The more valuable habit is the quarterly audit of twenty recent posts, which surfaces drift that is invisible day to day.

### Can AI maintain a consistent voice better than human writers?

In one specific respect, yes: a properly configured AI applies the same rules to every piece, whereas three human writers naturally produce three registers. What AI cannot do is exercise the judgment to break voice appropriately — knowing that a serious apology should drop the humour, for example. The reliable combination is AI for baseline consistency and a human editor for the exceptions.

## The Bottom Line

Brand voice is not a feeling. It is four specifiable dimensions, a vocabulary, a banned list, and a handful of examples.

Write that page once. Use it in every generation. Score drafts against it. Refresh the examples quarterly.

Do that and "make it sound like us" stops being a hope and becomes an instruction the tool can actually follow — which is the difference between AI content that embarrasses you and AI content that reads like your business.
