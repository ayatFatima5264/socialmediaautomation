There are two ways to use AI for content, and they produce wildly different results.

The first is asking a tool to write about a topic and publishing what comes back. This is what most people do, and it is why "AI content" has become a mild insult — the output is fluent, structurally tidy, and completely interchangeable with what your competitors are publishing.

The second is treating AI as a production system that you feed with your own material and control with a proper brief. This produces content that is fast to make *and* genuinely yours.

This guide covers the second approach end to end: the brief, the prompting, the brand voice, the editing pass, fact-checking, and the workflow that keeps quality high as volume increases.

## The Principle Everything Else Follows From

**AI is an amplifier of input quality, not a source of substance.**

Feed it a thin brief and a vague topic, and it fills the gap with the average of everything ever written on that subject — which is the definition of generic. Feed it your positioning, your customer's actual objection, and a real number from your business, and it produces something only you could have published.

Every technique below is really a variation on getting more of your own material into the input.

> If your AI content sounds generic, the problem is almost never the model. It is that you gave it nothing specific to work with.

## Part 1: The Foundation — Your Content Brief

Do this once. It improves every generation you make afterwards.

### What goes in it

- **Audience.** Specific, not a category. Not "small businesses" but "independent dental practices with two to five chairs, usually run by a practising dentist who does the marketing themselves."
- **What you sell**, and the three objections people raise before buying.
- **Content pillars** — three things you want to be known for. Everything you publish should map to one.
- **Tone in three words**, plus three words for what you are *not*. "Warm, plain-spoken, direct. Not corporate, not hype-driven, not cutesy."
- **Proof points.** Two or three real results, numbers, or customer stories you can reference.
- **Vocabulary rules.** Terms you use, terms you avoid. Most businesses have both and have never written them down.

### Why it matters more than prompting

People spend enormous effort on clever prompt wording and none on the brief. This is backwards. A mediocre prompt with a strong brief beats a brilliant prompt with no context, every time.

## Part 2: Prompting That Actually Works

Four techniques, in order of impact.

### 1. Supply the substance, ask for the structure

The highest-leverage pattern in all of AI content creation.

Instead of *"Write a post about customer retention"*, do this: dictate three minutes of unstructured thought about the retention problem you actually solved last quarter — what broke, what you tried, what the numbers were — and then ask AI to shape it into a post.

The substance is yours. The formatting labour disappears. The output is unrecognisable in quality compared to topic-based generation.

### 2. Ask for variations, not a single answer

Choosing between three options is faster than fixing one option, and it prevents you settling for the first thing you saw. Ask for five hooks, three angles, or three different tones.

### 3. Give it an example of your voice

Paste two of your own best-performing posts and say "match this register." This works better than any adjective you could supply, because voice is easier to demonstrate than describe.

### 4. Constrain the format explicitly

Vague requests produce vague output. Specify length, paragraph structure, whether you want a question at the end, and what to avoid. "Under 1,200 characters, paragraphs of one to three sentences, no rhetorical question in the opening, no phrases like 'in today's landscape'."

### What to stop doing

- **Elaborate role-play preambles.** "You are a world-class marketing expert with 20 years of experience" adds almost nothing compared to a good brief.
- **Asking for a finished post from a bare topic.** This is the source of nearly all generic output.
- **Accepting the first draft.** Regenerating with one specific correction is fast and usually a big improvement.

## Part 3: Brand Voice at Scale

Voice is where AI content most often fails, and where it is most fixable.

Define voice across four dimensions rather than with adjectives:

- **Formality** — do you say "we cannot" or "we can't"? First person or third?
- **Energy** — measured and calm, or punchy and urgent?
- **Warmth** — professional distance, or conversational and personal?
- **Directness** — do you hedge and qualify, or state things plainly?

Position yourself on each, write one example sentence demonstrating each, and store it. That document does more for consistency than any tool setting.

## Part 4: The Editing Pass

Never publish a first draft. But editing does not mean rewriting — it means five specific checks that take a few minutes.

**1. Cut the opening.** AI's first sentence is usually throat-clearing. Delete it and see whether the second sentence is a stronger start. It usually is.

**2. Add one specific detail.** A number, a name, a date, a real customer question. This single edit does more than everything else combined. It is also the thing AI cannot do for you.

**3. Verify every fact.** Any statistic, date, claim about how a platform works, or reference to a study. AI produces confident, plausible, non-existent figures routinely. If you cannot verify it, cut it.

**4. Remove the tells.** Rhetorical opening questions. "In today's fast-paced world." Tidy three-item lists where the third item is filler. Overuse of "moreover," "furthermore," and "it's important to note." Sentences that begin "Whether you're a X or a Y."

**5. Read the first two lines aloud.** If they sound like a press release, rewrite them the way you would say it to a colleague.

Five minutes per post. Non-negotiable.

## Part 5: Fact-Checking — The Part People Skip

This deserves its own section because it is the most common way AI content damages a business.

AI does not know when it is wrong. It produces a fabricated statistic with exactly the same confidence as a real one, and fabricated statistics are often *more* plausible-sounding than real ones because they are constructed to fit the argument.

**Rules that prevent problems:**

- **Never publish a number you have not personally traced to a source.** Not "it sounds about right" — actually found.
- **Prefer your own data.** "Our customers typically see X" is both safer and more valuable than a borrowed industry statistic.
- **Be suspicious of precision.** "73% of marketers report..." is the exact shape of a fabricated statistic.
- **Re-check platform claims.** How a network's features work changes frequently, and AI training data lags.
- **When unsure, hedge honestly.** "In our experience" and "typically" are perfectly good, and far better than a confident falsehood.

## Part 6: The Production Workflow

Putting it together, for a team producing regularly.

**Weekly capture (ongoing).** One running note. Every customer question, objection, result, and strong opinion goes in it. This is your substance supply, and it is what separates specific content from generic content.

**Monthly planning (10 minutes).** Generate a balanced calendar from your brief and pillars. Review at the topic level before anything is drafted — cheapest possible moment to fix problems.

**Batch drafting (20 minutes).** Generate everything in one pass, including platform variants. Do not edit while generating; the mode-switching is what makes sessions run long.

**Batch editing (15 minutes).** One pass, five checks per post, five minutes each for anything substantial.

**Visuals (10 minutes).** Generated imagery for concepts, real photography for anything where authenticity matters.

**Schedule and review (5 minutes).** Load the queue, then look at the whole calendar for clustering, repetition, and anything overtaken by events.

That is the [one-hour monthly session](/blog/create-month-of-social-media-posts-in-one-hour) in full.

## Part 7: Quality Control as You Scale

Volume is where AI content quality quietly degrades. Three guardrails.

**The interchangeability test.** Could a competitor publish this post with only their logo swapped? If yes, it is not worth publishing. Add something only you know.

**The pillar check.** Every piece should map to one of your three content pillars. Content that maps to nothing is content nobody asked for.

**The quarterly audit.** Every three months, read twenty of your own recent posts in one sitting. Drift is invisible day to day and obvious in bulk — you will spot repeated hooks, a tone that has slid, and pillars you have abandoned.

## Common Mistakes

- **No brief.** The root cause of most "AI sounds generic" complaints.
- **Topic-based generation.** Asking for a post about a subject rather than supplying material.
- **Publishing unedited.** Audiences recognise the patterns.
- **Unverified statistics.** The most damaging and most avoidable error.
- **Volume over relevance.** Ten generic posts underperform three specific ones.
- **Never revisiting the brief.** Your positioning changes; the document should too.
- **Using AI for the parts that need you.** Your opinion, your customer stories, your judgment about what to say. Those are the job.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does AI-generated content hurt SEO or social media reach?

Search engines and platforms target low-quality, unhelpful content rather than the tool used to produce it. Google's published guidance focuses on whether content demonstrates genuine usefulness and expertise regardless of how it was created. Edited, accurate, specific AI-assisted content performs normally; mass-produced generic content underperforms, as it always did.

### How do I stop AI content from sounding generic?

Two changes fix most of it. First, write a proper brief covering your audience, offers, tone, and proof points, and use it in every generation. Second, supply your own raw material — three minutes of unstructured thought about something that actually happened — and ask AI to structure it, rather than asking it to write about a topic. Generic output comes from generic input.

### Should I disclose that I use AI to create content?

There is no universal requirement for ordinary marketing content that you have reviewed and stand behind, and most businesses do not disclose the tools used to draft a caption any more than they disclose using a word processor. Disclosure matters more in contexts where authorship itself is the product — journalism, academic work, or where a platform or regulator specifically requires it. The obligation that always applies is accuracy.

### How much editing does AI content actually need?

Budget around five minutes per social post: cut the throat-clearing opening, add one specific detail, verify any facts, remove the recognisable AI phrasings, and check the opening reads like a person. That is editing, not rewriting. If you consistently need more than fifteen minutes, your brief is too thin or you are generating from bare topics.

### Can AI create good long-form content, not just social posts?

It can produce solid structure, outlines, and first drafts for long-form work, but the quality gap between generic and genuinely useful widens considerably at length. Long-form demands original research, real examples, and expertise that AI cannot supply. The effective pattern is the same as for social: you provide the substance and argument, AI handles structure and drafting speed.

## The Bottom Line

Good AI content creation is not about better prompts. It is about better input.

Write the brief once. Capture your own raw material continuously. Generate for structure, not substance. Edit in five specific ways. Verify every number. Audit quarterly.

Do that and AI becomes what it should be — a production system that lets you publish the things you already know, consistently, without the work that used to stop you.
